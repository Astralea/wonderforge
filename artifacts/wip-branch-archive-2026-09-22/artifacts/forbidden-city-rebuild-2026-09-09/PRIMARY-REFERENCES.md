# Forbidden City: initial official reference set

Research checked 2026-09-09. Preparation only: no scene/model changes. The recorded catalog selection is `forbidden-city`; this document does not repeat the draw.

## Five useful sources

1. **[UNESCO: Imperial Palaces of the Ming and Qing Dynasties](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/439/).** The Beijing palace was constructed in **1406–1420 under Zhu Di**. Use that construction interval, ending in 1420, rather than treating every surviving building as original. The official description establishes a central axis, symmetry, outer court in front and inner court behind. It explicitly values surviving architectural achievements particularly of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. This is the chronology/layout anchor, not a measured 1420 plan. Full page read.

2. **[Palace Museum: “重建太和殿”, 1695](https://www.dpm.org.cn/subject_600/buildingdetails/253792.html).** After the 1679 fire and lengthy material preparation, rebuilding began in 1695 and finished two years later, producing the present form. Therefore modern Supreme Harmony Hall photographs must not silently become an exact 1420 reconstruction. Official indexed article text read; direct page retrieval timed out. It also links specialist rebuilding/history essays for later, narrowly targeted investigation.

3. **[Palace Museum: 王文涛, “中和殿宝顶”](https://www.dpm.org.cn/Uploads/pdf/6179/T00120_00.pdf).** Useful silhouette comparison: the surviving Supreme Harmony Hall has a **double-eaved hipped roof**; Preserving Harmony has a **double-eaved hip-and-gable roof**; Central Harmony is the smaller central building with a pyramidal roof/finial. The article places Central Harmony’s founding in 1420 as **华盖殿**, then records fires and rebuilding, including the 1421 fire. Present eleven-/nine-bay descriptions are surviving-form references, not accepted Ming-original dimensions. Official indexed PDF text read; direct PDF retrieval timed out, so its illustrations have not been visually inspected in this pass.

4. **[Palace Museum: “Zero Waste at the Palace Museum” bilingual guidebook](https://young.dpm.org.cn/uploads/1/file/public/202505/20250516181259_97digy9q27.pdf).** Full 15-page PDF read. PDF page 3 contains a modern precinct tour map; pages 4–6 explain the site and materials. Red walls, yellow glazed tiles and upturned eaves provide a restrained palette/silhouette basis. Documented logistics: Yongle construction sourced scarce large timbers from southwestern China; excavated moat soil and demolished Yuan-palace debris contributed to Jingshan. Crucially, the guide distinguishes later Qing composite beams/columns and northeastern pine substitutions from earlier practice. Do not backdate these later examples. The tour map is an orientation aid, not a metric construction plan.

5. **[UNESCO: Buildings on the central axis of the Forbidden City](https://whc.unesco.org/en/documents/205926).** Official photograph record, **1 July 2019**, Jin Dongjun, © Beijing Municipal Cultural Heritage Bureau; 5472×3648 image available through the page. Useful for roof hierarchy, repeated courtyard rhythm and axis composition of the surviving complex. The record specifies attribution and no derivatives: keep it a linked visual reference, not a repainted/distributed texture. Metadata read; no image downloaded or model generated.

## Modeling implications and unresolved history

Start with an explicitly **interpretive 1406–1420 construction story**: earthworks and material yards, timber framing, roof structure and tiles, then a wide reveal of axial courtyards. These staging choices are design proposals, not source-proven work orders. Keep the monumental hipped, hip-and-gable and smaller pyramidal roof silhouettes distinct; avoid cloning one pavilion throughout.

The present-source references do **not** establish original hall spans, every 1420 roof detail, precise scaffolds, lifting rigs, worker numbers/clothing, cart designs or specific timber routes. No ice-road haul, enormous stone-sledge scene, exact vehicle fleet or labor count is admitted by this short pass. Those require dated evidence before depiction. Likewise, modern museum signage, Qing court furnishings, current gardens and reconstructed hall details must be separated from Ming chronology.

Before a claim of exact 1420 geometry, obtain a dated early plan/reconstruction argument. A contemporary photograph is useful for construction vocabulary and surviving silhouettes but cannot establish the original fabric by itself.
